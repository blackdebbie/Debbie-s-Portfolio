01 / The Starting Point

It started with a frustration.

I've spent time deep in the Base ecosystem — Coinbase's Layer 2 blockchain. Hundreds of projects launch every month: DeFi protocols, games, AI agents, mini apps. But there was no single place to find them, understand them, or know where to start.

If you were a crypto native, you relied on Twitter, Discord, and word of mouth. If you were new? You were completely lost. The promise of an open financial internet felt locked behind jargon, scattered links, and zero onboarding.

I wanted to build the front door. Not another protocol, not another token — a product where discovery and clarity were the entire point.

The Base ecosystem had a discoverability problem. No one had built the front door yet.

02 / The Problem

Four problems.
One product to solve them.

After mapping out the space and talking to users at different experience levels, four clear pain points emerged — not separate issues, but the same problem at four different stages of the user journey.

01
No discovery layer
New Base projects had no central home. Users relied entirely on scattered Twitter threads and community tips to find anything worth trying.
02
Incomprehensible projects
Most protocol websites assumed expert-level DeFi knowledge. Beginners had no idea what anything actually did or why they should care.
03
No action pathway
Even when users found a project, there were no clear next steps. "What do I actually do here?" had no answer.
04
Protocols lacked reach
New Base protocols had no good acquisition channel to reach engaged users willing to genuinely try their product.

03 / Research & Definition

Studying the ecosystem, not just the screen.

Before drawing a single wireframe, I audited existing platforms across ecosystems. I studied APTree on Aptos — a gamified rewards platform that drove users to complete DeFi tasks across partner protocols. It was a useful reference: it proved the model could work. But it revealed a gap I wanted to close.

APTree's moat was XP mechanics. NOOK's moat would be content quality — the clarity, depth, and usefulness of individual project pages.

Core insight
Every competitor treated XP as the product. I realized XP was just the reward. The real product was helping someone understand "what is this thing and how do I use it" — fast, in plain English, with zero assumptions about their knowledge level.

Three distinct user archetypes shaped every design decision:

A
Crypto Natives
Already active on Base. Want new protocol discovery and rewards for activity they're already doing.
B
Beginners
Curious but intimidated. Need plain-language explanations and hand-holding through their first on-chain action.
C
Cross-Chain Users
Active on Ethereum, Solana, or other chains. Evaluating whether Base deserves their attention.

04 / Strategy

A GTM move no one else was making.

Most discovery platforms in crypto wait for protocols to come to them — requiring partnerships before listing anything. That creates a chicken-and-egg problem: no traffic means no partners; no partners means no content.

I flipped the model. NOOK lists every Base project first, without needing permission. Build the directory, earn traffic organically, then approach protocols with real data in hand. That changes every conversation from a cold pitch to a warm proof point.

Phase 01
Build the directory
Free listings, no partnerships required. Earn organic traffic by being the most complete Base directory that exists.
Phase 02
Monetize with traction
Approach protocols with real traffic data. Introduce sponsored campaigns, paid placements, and featured listings.
Phase 03
XP becomes real
Once revenue exists, XP becomes redeemable. Never promised before there's money to back it — protecting user trust from day one.
Core principle
Don't promise users redeemable rewards until there's revenue to back it. XP starts as reputation and status only. This isn't a business constraint — it's a trust design decision.

05 / Design Process

From blank page
to working product.

The process ran in tight, pragmatic loops — define, design, build with static data, iterate. No waiting for a perfect backend before testing real UX decisions.

01
Information architecture
Mapped the full site structure before touching visual design. Five core sections with distinct purpose, distinct content type, and distinct user intent. No page was a duplicate of another.
02
Brand identity
Dark mode by default. Instrument Sans as primary font. Base blue (#0052FF) as the brand anchor. Glassmorphism and motion used deliberately, not decoratively. The animated eagle-eye SVG logo — blinks on hover — became the brand's signature detail.
03
Hero section
Designed to communicate one thing immediately: what you can do here. Staggered entrance animations — headlines slam in sequentially, the button scales up, decorative elements fan then float. Every motion had a purpose: pacing the user's attention, not showing off.
04
Reward Explorer
Built the core experience: an Ecosystem Explorer with four filter tabs (All, One-time task, Web-Apps, Mini-Apps) and initial Alpha partners like Aerodrome and Moonwell. Each section organizes tasks clearly, showing XP values and distinct calls-to-action right on the card.
05
Task Verification & SBTs
Integrated specific flows for varying task types. Social tasks (Follow, Like) lead directly to minting an early adopter SBT. On-chain tasks (Swap $10, Bridge) are designed to be verified automatically. We also implemented a Daily Check-in system with streaks to drive retention.
06
Navigation & global UX
The navbar starts fully transparent with white text, transitions to a dark blurred background on scroll. A detail that makes the hero feel seamless and expansive. The profile avatar links to the user's profile, keeping the experience fluid after wallet connection.
07
AI chatbot — the ecosystem guide
A floating AI assistant positioned as a Base ecosystem guide, not a generic chatbot. Suggestion chips guide users toward useful questions. Web search built in for real-time protocol info. Placed globally in layout.tsx so it persists across all pages without reloading.
08
Profile page
Wallet-gated with avatar upload, an edit modal, a stats row, portfolio snapshot, and badge display. Solved an SSR hydration bug using a mounted state pattern — a reminder that clean designs still hit rough technical edges in Next.js.

06 / Key Design Decisions

The calls that shaped
what NOOK became.

Good product design isn't just about what you build. It's about what you refuse to build, and why.

Clarity over gamification
XP is a reward mechanism, not the product. Project pages that make Base accessible to anyone are the real differentiator. Discovery and understanding come first — always.
No referral system
Scrapped completely to protect the XP economy. Referrals invite farming and abuse before a single protocol partnership is signed. Organic growth only.
Categorized task flows
One-time social tasks (minting an SBT) and recurring partner tasks (Web-Apps vs Mini-Apps) are kept strictly separate to prevent cognitive overload for beginners.
Static data first
Consciously accepted hardcoded project data while the backend is being built. Speed to test the UX mattered more than backend completeness at this stage.
CSS Modules over Tailwind
New components use CSS Modules for consistency with the existing codebase, preventing style drift across a growing component library.
Dark/light mode from day one
System preference detection, localStorage persistence, CSS custom properties. Not a nice-to-have — a core requirement for a crypto-native audience.

07 / Learnings

What this project taught me.

NOOK is still in active development, but the design decisions made early have already defined what kind of product it's becoming — and what kind it's refusing to be.

01
Content strategy is product strategy
The decision to make individual project pages NOOK's moat wasn't a design decision — it was a business one. In Web3, where everything looks the same, the product that helps people actually understand things wins.
02
Traffic before partnerships changes power dynamics
By listing all Base projects without permission first, NOOK removes the single biggest GTM blocker for a new platform. Good design creates leverage, not just delight.
03
Phased promises are a design principle
Not promising redeemable XP until revenue exists is a trust design decision. What you don't ship matters as much as what you do. Restraint in feature scope is a form of craft.
04
Technical constraints sharpen design thinking
Designing for an 8GB RAM machine forced thinking about performance as a design value. Downgrading to Next.js 15.1 to avoid memory crashes wasn't a setback — it was a constraint that made the product more disciplined.

08 / What's Next

The road ahead.

The next milestone is connecting NOOK to a real backend — Supabase for the database, Alchemy for on-chain verification, and a task system that works across three verification methods: on-chain, partner API, and manual review.

  • Supabase backend — user wallets, XP balances, completed tasks, daily check-in streaks, and events
  • On-chain task verification — Alchemy webhooks monitoring user wallet transactions against task criteria in real time
  • NOOK Events — paid and sponsored campaigns with XP or prizes, the primary revenue stream once organic traffic is established
  • Leaderboard — a reputation layer that gives XP social meaning before it becomes redeemable
  • Protocol partnership outreach — approaching Base protocols with real traffic data instead of cold pitches
